Model answer ## Electric charge is the property of matter that makes it experience a force in an electric field; it is of two kinds, positive and negative, measured in coulombs (C). Objects are electrified by friction, conduction and induction. Experiment: rub a glass rod with silk and an ebonite rod with fur; each rod then picks up small pieces of paper, and a hanging glass rod is repelled by another rubbed glass rod but attracted by a rubbed ebonite rod. This shows two kinds of charge: like charges repel and unlike charges attract. In friction, electrons move from one material to the other, so one becomes positive and the other negative; in conduction a charged body touching a neutral one shares its charge with it.

Bring a negatively charged rod near, but not touching, an insulated neutral metal sphere: electrons in the sphere are repelled to the far side, so the near side becomes positive and the far side negative. Model answer ## Coulomb's law: the electric force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, $F = k\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$ with $k = 9\times10^{9}$ N m² C⁻². The force acts along the line joining the charges; it is attractive for unlike charges and repulsive for like charges. Doubling one charge doubles the force; doubling the distance reduces the force to one quarter.

Model answer ## In a series combination the capacitors are connected end to end so that the same charge $Q$ is on each, while the potential differences add up, $V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$. Model answer ## Electromagnetic induction is the production of an emf (and current) in a conductor when the magnetic flux linked with it changes (Faraday). Factors: (1) the speed of relative motion of the magnet and coil (faster change gives larger emf); (2) the strength of the magnet; (3) the number of turns of the coil; (4) the area of the coil. Model answer ## Lenz's law gives the direction of the induced emf: it acts in such a direction that the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it (the minus sign in Faraday's law). For example, pushing the north pole of a magnet into a coil makes the near end of the coil a north pole, which repels the magnet; work has to be done against this repulsion, and that work appears as electrical energy. If the induced current helped the change, energy would be created from nothing, so Lenz's law is an expression of conservation of energy.
