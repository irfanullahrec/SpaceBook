Her husband mocked: "Yes, yes, make a great fuss — tears are always resting on your eyelashes. Have you ever asked how I am, what worries I have at the office, that the house loan hangs over my head? Madam wants me to sit at home gazing at her face and singing her praises." The Begum cried out, "There is a limit — stop it! He will sing my praises?" and held back her tears. Her husband told everyone, "It's simple: she is fed up with me. I have bought a ticket; let her stay a month or two with her sister — it isn't even cold in Karachi. Aunt, you'll look after the children," and picked up a samosa and nibbled it. The Begum broke down sobbing: why should she go to someone else's house, leaving her own home and children — and to her younger sister's in-laws at that? The neighbour lady said that going to one's brother's or father's house is fine, but to a sister's in-laws…? The aunt said women these days were something else; when they were fed up, they would gather up the children and go as guests to a sister, brother, aunt — whoever they liked — and after a while the husband would come himself to coax them back; their mother used to say that is how a wife's worth is recognised. The Begum said, "He wants to be rid of me. What have I not done for him?
