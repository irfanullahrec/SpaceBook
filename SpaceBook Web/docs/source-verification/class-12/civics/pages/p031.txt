In the authoritarian period the executive mostly remained non-political. Powers remained in the hands of martial-law administrators or ruling military generals. In this system the members of the executive remained powerless, because in these systems powers were severely centralised. The executive mostly consisted of the members of the Convention Party (the ruler’s party), or of the bureaucracy, big traders, jagirdars and industrialists etc. In this system the attitude of the executive always remained authoritarian. The authoritarian administration remained answerable to one individual and worked as the personal employees of that ruler. In this kind of executive there was no collective or public answerability. In the first democratic period (1947–1958), the members of the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan who had been elected in the elections held before independence, in the British Indian Parliament, and who were representatives of the areas of Pakistan; later a central legislature was elected through the provincial assemblies. In the democratic period the representatives were truly elected representatives of the people. The legislature worked for constitution-making and democratic law-making. Despite many weaknesses, the legislature of that period worked in a democratic manner and enjoyed public confidence and support. In the democratic period (1971–1977), as a result of Pakistan’s first general elections, an elected legislature came into being, which, by the consensus of public wishes and political parties, approved the Constitution of 1973. In the third period (1988–1999) the elected political democratic legislature kept working. Public opinion was respected, and despite some defects the legislature adopted a democratic style.
