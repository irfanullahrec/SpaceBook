For him, although none of the scholars and poets came forward, when they brought him in, the hereditary kings looked at him with contempt and smiled. The group of historians, however, made a great fanfare. When the word “genealogy” came from someone’s tongue, he at once presented as his credential a jewelled sword on which was written in letters of blood: “In kingship there is no inheritance.” The scholars raised an outcry that he from whose clothes comes the smell of blood is a butcher; he has no place among kings. The poets said: “He whose picture the pen of our writings, or the painters of compositions, have not coloured with the colour of permanence, we shall not let come into this court.” At this he too hesitated and appeared regretful. At that moment a voice from the unseen called: “O Genghis! Just as you stirred the zeal of conquest and the sword in your nation’s blood, if you had also cared for the sciences and arts, then today, by virtue of the nation’s sympathy, you would not have suffered such a failure.” Meanwhile a few historians came forward. They showed some leaves in which the Tora of Genghis Khan — that is, the rules of his administration of the country — were written. His name was Halaku Khan. For him a few scholars too joined the historians. When they brought him in, an uproar of disputes was about to arise for him too, but an elderly man took his hand and brought him forward, whose manner was that of orthodox scholars but at whose waist hung on one side an astrolabe and on the other some figures of Euclid. Under his arm were some sheets of philosophy and wisdom. His name was Muhaqqiq Tusi. So, seeing him, no one could speak.
