While signing, his hand twisted and writhed, and as he drew each circle, big and small, the roundness of his open mouth grew and shrank so that the onlooker’s eye was dazzled. In those days the Khan’s account was in the Muslim Commercial Bank, Chowk Yadgar branch, where those who signed in Urdu had to give, on stamp paper, an insulting “indemnity” (guarantee) that if a fraud occurred in their account because of forged signatures the bank would not be responsible — rather, if the bank suffered any loss as a result, directly or indirectly, they would make it good. When I make my own signature with such difficulty, how can another make it? You must have two dozen men on your staff; all of them look like thieves, rogues and swindlers. If any of them forges my signature and shows it to me, I’ll give him a thousand rupees reward on the spot — and then shoot him dead.” Mr McLean said he could not change the bank’s rules; the same rule held at Grindlays Bank — “we copied all our forms from it, fly for fly; on this form, through the printer’s carelessness, even Grindlays Bank’s name is printed.” “Khan! Learn to sign in English and you’ll be free of this bother.” To give his order a colour of entreaty, he treated the Khan to tea and pastry. In obedience the Khan practised English signatures for two months; when they were quite fluent and firm, he lifted the curtain, walked straight into Mr McLean’s room and signed in his presence — first raising his hand and signing four or five times in the air, then suddenly putting the pen on paper and signing at full speed.
