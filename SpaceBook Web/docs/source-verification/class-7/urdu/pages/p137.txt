The patient was restless with pain and had not the courage to answer every question, but the doctor kept smiling. At last, when despite two or three refusals he shook the molar well and saw that the patient was about to faint from the severity of the pain, he was sure it really was toothache. He then pointed to the chair and began boiling two or three instruments in hot water. The patient said, "Please hurry, pull my molar out with electricity"; he said, "Today the electricity has failed so the molar will have to be taken out by hand." While the instruments heated he lectured on diseases arising from bad teeth; in his view all the diseases of the world arise from bad teeth, and in the lecture he also commented on this country's habits. When the instruments were hot, the doctor said, "Now there will be an injection." The patient has disliked the very word since the first day because in his view an injection is another name for inflicting pain in a civilised way. The doctor assured him no pain would come, but this was not true because it hurt a good deal. A few minutes after the injection when he took the forceps the patient saw angels of death; the doctor gripped the molar and the patient felt the time had come to give last wishes to friends and relatives. Then a regular wrestling began between tooth and forceps; the molar stood firm like the Qutb Minar that has stood in place despite thousands of earthquakes. In this tug-of-war the patient was being slaughtered for free; at last the settlement between forceps and tooth was that half the molar stay in the forceps' mouth and half in the patient's.
