qp 2 | Explain the grammatical concepts of Relative Pronouns, Indefinite Pronouns, and Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement with examples.
sol Solution ## Marks: 8 Marks · Outline: 1. Overview of Pronouns in English Grammar. 2. Relative Pronouns: Definition, Functions, and Examples (who, which, that, whose, where). 3. Indefinite Pronouns: Definition and Usage (someone, anyone, nothing, everyone). 4. Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement Rules (Number and Gender consistency). 5. Importance in Clear Academic Writing. · Answer: Pronouns are essential grammatical tools that replace nouns to prevent repetitive and clumsy sentence structures:  1. Relative Pronouns: Relative pronouns connect a dependent relative clause to an antecedent noun: • Who / Whom: Used for persons (e.g. *The teacher who inspired me received an award*). • Which: Used for animals and non-living things in non-restrictive clauses (e.g. *My car, which is old, runs well*). • That: Used for persons or things in restrictive clauses (e.g. *The book that you lent me is excellent*). • Whose: Shows possession (e.g. *The student whose project won was delighted*). • Where / When / Why: Indicate place, time, and reason.  2. Indefinite Pronouns: Indefinite pronouns refer to non-specific persons, places, or things: • Singular: *someone, anyone, everyone, no one, something, anything, nothing, each*. • Plural: *both, few, several, many*. • Example: *Is there anyone who can solve this problem?*  3. Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement: A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in both number (singular/plural) and gender (masculine/feminine/neuter): • Correct: *Saima completed her assignment.* (Singular feminine) • Correct: *The boys gathered their books.* (Plural)  Mastery of these rules ensures clarity, cohesion, and precision in academic writing and board examinations.
qp 3 | Elucidate the concepts of Anaphoric and Cataphoric references, and explain how prefixes and suffixes alter word meanings.
sol Solution ## Marks: 8 Marks · Outline: 1. Cohesion at Discourse Level: Textual References. 2. Anaphoric References (Looking Backward) with Examples. 3. Cataphoric References (Looking Forward) with Examples. 4. Morphology: Prefixes and their Semantic Functions (un-, in-, pre-, dis-, mis-). 5. Suffixes and Grammatical Class Shifts (-less, -able, -ful, -ness, -ment). 6. Summary Table. · Answer: Cohesive devices and word-formation mechanics form the bedrock of sophisticated reading comprehension and writing proficiency:  1. Anaphoric Reference (Backward Pointing): An anaphoric reference occurs when a word refers back to an antecedent that was already mentioned earlier in the text: • Example: *Allama Iqbal was a great philosopher. He inspired millions.* ('He' refers back to 'Allama Iqbal'). • Function: Creates smooth textual continuity without repeating proper nouns.  2. Cataphoric Reference (Forward Pointing): A cataphoric reference occurs when a word refers forward to a noun that appears later in the text: • Example: *Although she was tired, Fatima finished her homework.* ('She' points forward to 'Fatima'). • Function: Builds dramatic anticipation and rhetorical emphasis.  3. Prefixes (Meaning Modifiers): A prefix is attached to the beginning of a root word to alter its meaning: • Un- (not): *believable → unbelievable* • In- (not): *correct → incorrect* • Pre- (before): *view → preview* • Dis- (opposite / removal): *appear → disappear* • Mis- (wrongly): *understand → misunderstand*  4. Suffixes (Class & Meaning Shifters): A suffix is added to the end of a root word, often changing its grammatical part of speech: • -less (without): *rest → restless (adj.)* • -able (capable of): *adore → adorable (adj.)* • -ful (full of): *thank → thankful (adj.)* • -ness (state of being): *kind → kindness (noun)* • -ment (process/result): *agree → agreement (noun)*  Understanding these linguistic tools empowers students to expand vocabulary and analyze complex texts efficiently.

qp 5 | Explain the Morphological Formation and Semantic Roles of Prefixes and Suffixes with Textbook Illustrations from Unit 2.
sol Solution ## Marks: 8 Marks · Outline: I. Definition of Affixation: Derivational vs. Inflectional Morphology II. Analysis of Negative Prefixes (un-, dis-, im-, in-, non-) III. Analysis of Noun/Adjective Suffixes (-ness, -ful, -ment, -less, -able) IV. Synthesizing Root Words and Affixes in English Sentence Construction · Answer: Affixation is the primary morphological process of word formation in English.  1. Prefixes (Morphemes added to the front of a base root): - 'un-' (reversal/negation): predictable -> unpredictable, certain -> uncertain. - 'dis-' (negation/separation): honest -> dishonest, courage -> discourage. - 'im-' / 'in-' (negation): possible -> impossible, active -> inactive. - 'mis-' (wrongly): guide -> misguide, lead -> mislead.  2. Suffixes (Morphemes added to the end of a base root): - '-ful' (full of -> forms adjectives): hope -> hopeful, beauty -> beautiful. - '-less' (without -> forms adjectives): hope -> hopeless, harm -> harmless. - '-ment' (action/state -> forms nouns): achieve -> achievement, develop -> development. - '-ness' (state/quality -> forms abstract nouns): bright -> brightness, dark -> darkness.  Mastery of affixes expands vocabulary exponentially and ensures syntactic versatility.
