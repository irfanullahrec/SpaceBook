p Actual heat engines have dissipative effects, reducing efficiency. In 1824 Sadi Carnot considered a hypothetical ideal engine without heat losses or friction, called a Carnot heat engine; every actual engine is less efficient. A Carnot engine consists of a gas cylinder with perfectly insulating walls and perfectly conducting base, and a perfectly insulated, weightless, frictionless piston. 10.11.1 CARNOT CYCLE: a heat-engine cycle is complete when system properties return to their original state. The most efficient engine’s operating cycle, the Carnot cycle, has four processes: two isothermal and two adiabatic. (Figure 10.13) P–V diagram labels: pressure P, volume V, states A, B, C, D, upper isotherm at T₁ with Q₁, lower isotherm at T₂ with Q₂, and adiabats connecting B–C and D–A. 1. ISOTHERMAL EXPANSION: The working gas begins at pressure P₁, volume V₁ and temperature T₁. Place the cylinder on a high-temperature reservoir at T₁. The gas absorbs heat +Q₁ while its temperature remains constant; volume increases and pressure decreases. The state changes to P₂,V₂,T₁ with absorbed heat +Q₁; the isothermal expansion is path A–B on Figure 10.13. 2. ADIABATIC EXPANSION: Place the cylinder on an insulating stand, so no heat enters or leaves. Let the gas expand from V₂ to V₃; temperature falls from T₁ to T₂ and pressure falls from P₂ to P₃. This is the adiabatic expansion along B–C.
