p Carnot-engine efficiency depends on the temperatures of the high- and low-temperature reservoirs and is independent of the working substance. If the cold reservoir were at 0 K, equation (10.15) would give η=1−0/T₁=1 or 100%, so all Q₁ would become work, contradicting Kelvin’s second-law statement. Therefore absolute zero cannot be attained. The theoretical efficiency of an ideal engine is always less than 100%; a real engine cannot attain Carnot efficiency because of friction and heat losses by conduction and radiation. Carnot’s theorem: the most efficient engine depends on the two reservoir temperatures and not on the working substance; no real heat engine between two reservoirs is more efficient than a Carnot engine operating between the same reservoirs. For your information: the first successful petrol engine was invented by Nikolaus Otto in 1876; the Diesel engine was invented by Rudolph Diesel in 1892. Example 10.5—Carnot heat engine: A Carnot engine has maximum efficiency 55%; it takes heat from a source, converts part into work and rejects the remainder to the sink. If source temperature T₁=600 K, find sink temperature T₂. Given: T₁=600 K; η=55%=0.55.
